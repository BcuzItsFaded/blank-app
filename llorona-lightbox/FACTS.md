# Facts and sources

La Llorona is a folk legend, not documented history. The film frames every claim as legend, hypothesis or variant ("some researchers", "according to a famous legend", "historians have never confirmed"). The narration is the author's script.

| Claim in the film | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| The legend is older than the colonial drowning story; ancient Mexico told of a weeping woman | Widely cited; the pre-Hispanic link is a scholarly hypothesis, not settled fact | Library of Congress Folklife blog, "La Llorona: Roots, Branches, and the Missing Link from Spain" (https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2021/10/la-llorona-roots-branches-and-the-missing-link-from-spain/); History Today, "The Wailing Woman" (https://www.historytoday.com/miscellanies/wailing-woman) |
| Before the conquest, an omen described a woman wandering at night crying for her children | Documented as the sixth of the ten omens of the conquest in Sahagún's Florentine Codex; a weeping woman heard at night: "O my children, we are about to go forever" | Florentine Codex (Sahagún), as summarized by the Library of Congress blog above |
| Researchers connect the cries to Cihuacóatl, an Aztec goddess associated with motherhood | A commonly drawn scholarly comparison (Cihuacóatl is described in the codex as a white-clad figure who wails at night); it is an inference, not proven descent | The Collector, "The Terrifying True Origins of La Llorona" (https://www.thecollector.com/terrifying-true-origins-latin-americas-weeping-woman-llorona/); Grunge, "The Legend of La Llorona Explained" (https://grunge.com/218327/the-legend-of-la-llorona-explained/) |
| The later tale: a woman (often "María") betrayed in love drowns her children in a river and is doomed to wander, crying "¡Ay, mis hijos!" | Folklore; the most widespread modern version. "María" and the details vary by region | Grunge and The Collector above; godsandmonsters.info, "La Llorona" (https://godsandmonsters.info/la-llorona/) |
| Historians have not confirmed the tragedy; different regions tell different versions | Accurate: the exact lineage is uncertain and regionally varied | Library of Congress blog; History Today above |
| The surviving warning: do not follow the crying near water | The legend's enduring cautionary function | Folklore (general) |

The names, dates and the "María" story vary between sources and are presented in the film as legend. No gore is shown; the drowning is suggested only through light and water. Cihuacóatl's glyph and the "goddess" figure are illustrative, not reconstructions of a specific codex image.
