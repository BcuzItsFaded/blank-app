# What Did Ancient Humans Actually Do All Day?
**Channel:** Ink Explainer · **Video:** https://youtu.be/49_Ph2q6uIM · **Length:** ~9 min · **Transcript source:** sozai.app mirror (YouTube blocked direct caption access)

## TL;DR
The video argues that hunter-gatherers worked far fewer hours than modern people,
roughly 15–20 hours a week to get food. They spent the rest of their time on
relationships, craft, music and fireside storytelling. Farming, which began about
10,000 years ago, set off a population "trap" that raised the workload, made people
less healthy, and eventually led to the idea that a person's hours can be sold to
someone else.

## Key takeaways
- Skeletons from the Middle East suggest hunter-gatherers were taller and had healthier teeth and joints than the early farmers who followed them. The farmers had cavities and arthritis, and they died younger.
- Studies of foragers (Ju/'hoansi, Hadza, Aché, Martu) put food-getting work at about 15–20 hours a week.
- "Idle" social time worked as insurance: being liked meant being fed when you couldn't hunt.
- Talk around the fire at night was mostly stories, myths and jokes. The video presents this as the birthplace of culture.
- People before electric light probably slept in two halves, with a calm waking period in the middle of the night.
- Farming → more food → more people → more fields → more work, with no way back. Specialization, trade, cities, taxes and wage labour followed.

## Section by section
### 00:00 — Modern life vs ancient freedom
It opens by comparing a modern alarm clock with an ancient person waking up and asking, "What should I do today?"
### 00:40 — Two skeletons
Two skeletons from the same region, a few thousand years apart. Viewers usually guess the healthy one is the farmer, but it's the forager.
### 01:59 — A normal day
A natural wake-up, leftovers by the fire, a plan agreed by the group, and gathering or tool and hide work. Everything needed is done by early afternoon.
### 03:21 — How hunting really worked
Persistence hunting: following prey at a walk for hours until it overheats and collapses. It's the hardest day's work, but one kill feeds the camp for days.
### 03:56 — Richard Lee in the Kalahari (1963)
Lee timed Ju/'hoansi activities and found about 17 hours a week spent getting food.
### 04:32 — Other groups
The Hadza (Tanzania), Aché (Paraguay) and Martu (Australia) show similar 15–20 hour figures.
### 05:20 — Afternoons: social time and craft
Grooming, play, conversation and decoration. James Suzman reports about 6 hours a day of social time among the Ju/'hoansi.
### 05:58 — Beads and flutes
Shell beads at Blombos Cave, South Africa (70,000+ years old), and a vulture-bone flute found in Germany in 2008 (about 40,000 years old, five finger holes).
### 06:36 — Relationships as the safety net
With no money or institutions, other people served as bank, hospital and pension.
### Night — Fire and stories
Polly Wiessner (2014): daytime Ju/'hoansi talk was practical, while about 81% of firelight talk was storytelling.
### Night — Two-part sleep
About 4 hours of sleep, an hour or two awake, then about 4 more. The video suggests waking at 2 a.m. may be normal rather than insomnia.
### ~10,000 years ago — The farming trap
In the Fertile Crescent, farming fed population growth that locked people in. Workload rose, health declined, and specialists, cities and taxes followed.
### Close — Where your time went
Time was traded away over generations, not stolen. The video notes we spend about 90,000 hours of life working, chasing a retirement that foragers lived every day.

## People, studies & numbers cited
- **Richard Lee** (1963, Ju/'hoansi, Kalahari): about 17 hrs/week on food.
- **Hadza, Aché, Martu** follow-up studies: 15–20 hrs/week.
- **James Suzman**: about 6 hrs/day of social time (Ju/'hoansi).
- **Polly Wiessner** (2014, *PNAS*): 81% of firelight talk was stories.
- **Blombos Cave** shell beads: 70,000+ years old.
- **Bone flute, Germany** (found 2008): about 40,000 years old.
- Lifetime work: about 90,000 hours.

## Caveats
- The 15–20 hour figure usually counts **food-getting only**. When food processing, tool-making and housework are included (as some re-analyses of Lee's data do), totals rise to roughly 40+ hours a week. That is still varied work with little hierarchy, but the gap is smaller than the video implies.
- Modern foragers live in marginal environments and have had contact with farmers, so they are imperfect stand-ins for Paleolithic people.
- The skeleton comparison is a well-documented trend (shorter stature, more dental disease after farming), but outcomes varied by region.
- "Segmented sleep" rests mainly on historical European records and one lab study (Wehr, 1990s). Studies of modern foragers mostly find a single block of sleep.
- The video is a persuasive essay. Its framing of farming as a "trap" is one interpretation (popularized by Jared Diamond and Yuval Noah Harari), not settled fact.
