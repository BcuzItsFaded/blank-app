Street Fighter gives us fireballs, a shady tournament, a demonic threat, and a villain who sees the world's best fighters as his personal power supply. That sounds like everything you need for a wild video-game movie. But having all the right characters doesn't automatically mean you have a great story. Spoilers ahead. Let's get into what works, what doesn't, and the plot behind all those punches.
The movie opens with an animated introduction to Ryu and Ken, two fighters who trained under the same master, Gouken, and became almost like brothers. But their lives go in completely different directions. Ryu withdraws from the spotlight, while Ken becomes a celebrity who still can't escape the feeling that his old friend was the better fighter.
That's a stronger starting point than simply asking who can punch hardest. Ken has the fame, but Ryu has the talent Ken can't stop measuring himself against.
And Ryu isn't hiding just because he dislikes attention. His past involves a confrontation with Akuma, his master's death, and his refusal to embrace the darker power inside himself. So returning to competition means facing something much bigger than an old rivalry.
Meanwhile, M. Bison organizes a tournament, but finding the greatest fighter isn't his real objective. He wants to drain the fighters' supernatural energy to increase his own power. Ryu, with his ability to channel Hado, is an especially valuable target.
Then there's Chun-Li. She appears to be helping Bison recruit competitors, but she's actually an undercover Interpol agent seeking revenge for her father's murder. The tournament is really a meeting point for several different agendas.
The most promising part is Ryu and Ken's relationship. Ryu is controlled and serious; Ken is restless, loud, and desperate to prove himself. Their personalities naturally clash, and the hotel-room fight gives that rivalry a chance to become physical comedy, not just another battle with the fate of the world attached.
That's the kind of scene this adaptation seems best suited for: recognizable characters, clear personalities, and action that tells us something about their relationship.
Another welcome surprise is Dan. He initially looks like the opportunistic agent hanging around Ken for money, but his genuine loyalty gives the movie an emotional connection beyond the central rivalry. In a film crowded with powerful fighters, the guy who's clearly out of his depth can still matter.
The frustrating part is that the tournament doesn't really deliver as the backbone of the story. Several bouts happen off-screen or disappear through forfeits. When your premise is a gathering of the world's greatest fighters, skipping the competition feels like leaving the best part of the arcade machine unplugged.
Ken's emotional journey also needs a clearer direction. Is he fighting to beat Ryu, honor their master, or finally feel good enough? Those motives could work together, but the writing doesn't connect them firmly enough to give his later struggles their full impact.
Ryu has the opposite problem: a potentially powerful internal conflict that gets explained through repeated animated flashbacks. The animation makes a strong first impression, but returning to it so often interrupts the momentum instead of letting the present-day story carry the emotion.
And as the movie gets bigger, the effects increasingly compete with the martial arts. The human skill is part of the attraction. If every confrontation becomes a pileup of supernatural spectacle, the individual hits start to feel less important.
Street Fighter's biggest strength is its willingness to embrace how ridiculous this universe can be. Its biggest weakness is assuming that enthusiasm can do the work of storytelling. There's a compelling movie somewhere inside Ryu's fear of his own power, Ken's insecurity, and their damaged friendship. But the crowded roster and scattered plotting keep pulling attention away from that core.
Based on the published reviews, the verdict is mixed: an energetic, sometimes funny adaptation with memorable character moments, but not the decisive victory fans might have hoped for. IGN's 6 out of 10 captures that middle ground.
A strong roster. Some good hits. But the story still needs another round.
