# The Dancing Plague of 1518 — factual notes

Episode 19 of **PLOT TWIST WITH A&R** (paper-cut lightbox, ~62s, English).

## What is well documented
- **Strasbourg, summer 1518** (then a free imperial city of the Holy Roman Empire,
  today in France). In **mid-July 1518** a woman usually named **Frau Troffea**
  began dancing in the street and kept going for days. This is recorded in
  contemporary city council records, chronicles, and sermons. The card
  "FRAU TROFFEA · JULY 1518" follows the standard account.
- **It spread.** Within about a week dozens more were dancing. Some accounts give
  roughly 50 people by late July, and later ones say up to about 400 over the summer.
- **Physicians' explanation.** Local physicians ruled out supernatural causes and
  blamed a natural illness of "hot blood". The narration's "summer heat and the
  stars" reflects the humoral and astrological medicine of the time.
- **The authorities' response.** The council first did the opposite of a ban:
  believing the dancers had to dance it out, they opened guild halls, built a
  stage, and **hired musicians**. That is why the score is a pipe-and-tabor dance.
  When this made things worse, they **banned public dancing and music**
  (drums and tambourines were forbidden; quieter stringed music was allowed only at
  weddings and first masses). The edict and the
  struck-out drum show this.
- **Saint Vitus.** Many believed it was a curse of Saint Vitus. Sufferers were
  taken to his shrine (a grotto chapel near Saverne, in the Vosges) in hope of a
  cure. The hilltop shrine and pilgrimage show this.

## What is disputed
- **Deaths.** Later accounts claim many deaths, with figures like 15 a day. Historians
  have not found contemporary records confirming deaths, and the death toll is
  debated. The film marks it "DEATHS · DISPUTED" and uses the danse macabre
  only as a period image.
- **The cause.** Leading theories are stress-induced mass psychogenic illness
  (famine, disease and hardship, plus a strong local belief in Saint Vitus's
  curse; John Waller's view) and ergot poisoning (widely doubted, since ergotism
  causes convulsions and restricted blood flow, not days of coordinated dancing).
  It remains unresolved, as the ending says.

## Artistic licence
- Silhouette figures, the red shoes, the zodiac wheel and the skeleton are
  illustrative motifs, not depictions of specific records.
